*Summarise the project's aims and objectives:*

BACKGROUND & JUSTIFICATION

Dementia is a progressive condition that affects memory, thinking and communication. Detecting it early, and distinguishing it from milder changes known as mild cognitive impairment (MCI), is clinically important but difficult, because the early signs are subtle and overlap with normal ageing. MCI is especially hard to classify, as it sits between healthy cognition and dementia.

Changes in how a person speaks can be an early and sensitive signal of cognitive decline. Two simple, well-established speaking tasks are widely used in cognitive assessment: a verbal fluency task, in which a person names as many words as they can beginning with a given letter within a time limit; and a picture-description task (the standard "Cookie Theft" picture), in which a person describes a visual scene. Features of how someone performs these tasks, such as the words they produce, their pauses, and how they organise their description, carry information about cognitive health.

Automatic classifiers can be trained to analyse these speech features and predict whether a person is healthy, has MCI, or has dementia. However, such systems typically give a single prediction without explaining their reasoning, which limits how far clinicians can trust or act on them. There is growing interest in tools that instead present speech information in an interpretable way to support, rather than replace, clinical judgement.

AIM

This project will develop and evaluate a clinician-facing "decision support dashboard" that presents interpretable speech features from the two tasks above in a clear visual form, to help clinicians reason about whether a case is healthy, MCI or dementia. Importantly, the dashboard presents information for the clinician to interpret, keeping the clinician in control of the decision.

The research will evaluate how clinicians perform when using the dashboard, and compare their diagnostic decisions against the confirmed diagnosis for each case ("ground truth") and against automated classifiers, to understand the relative strengths of human and machine judgement, particularly for the difficult MCI group.

OBJECTIVES

1\. Co-design: gather input from clinicians through a focus group to shape the dashboard's design and content.  
2\. Refine: obtain structured feedback on a full draft of the dashboard and make final changes before evaluation.  
3\. Evaluate: ask clinicians experienced in dementia assessment to review a set of previously-collected, de-identified cases using the dashboard, recording for each case their diagnostic decision (healthy / MCI / dementia), their confidence, and the time taken.  
4\. Measure usability and workload: assess the dashboard using standard questionnaires on usability, mental workload and trust, alongside open-ended feedback.  
5\. Benchmark: compare clinicians' diagnostic accuracy against automated classifiers and against the confirmed diagnoses.  
6\. Stretch objectives (resources permitting): extend the evaluation to primary care clinicians (e.g. GPs and nurse practitioners) to test whether the dashboard supports non-specialists; and incorporate an additional speech task (semantic fluency).

WHAT THE STUDY INVOLVES

Participants are qualified clinicians. The patient speech data presented within the dashboard was collected previously under a separate ethics approval \[reference number: NIHR202911\] whose consent provisions permit use in future research; it is de-identified, and no new patient data is collected in this study. The present application therefore concerns only the involvement of clinician participants.

*\* Summarise the project's methodology*

OVERVIEW

This is a mixed-methods study carried out in three phases: a co-design focus group, a feedback round on a full draft of the dashboard, and a main evaluation study in which clinicians use the dashboard to review cases. All research activities involve qualified clinicians and researchers only. No new patient data is collected; the patient speech data shown in the dashboard was collected previously by the CognoSpeak group under a separate ethics approval that permits future research use, and is de-identified.

The first two phases will be carried out with members of the CognoSpeak group, an interdisciplinary research team at the University of Sheffield comprising computer scientists, neuroscience researchers at the Sheffield Institute for Translational Neuroscience (SITraN), and affiliated clinicians at Sheffield Teaching Hospitals. This group developed the original system used to collect the speech data, and the lead researchers are members of the group. Drawing the co-design and feedback participants from this team means they bring directly relevant expertise in both the underlying technology and clinical assessment.

PARTICIPANT SEPARATION

To protect the integrity of the evaluation, members who take part in the co-design or feedback phases will not take part in the main evaluation study, as prior involvement in the design would make them unrepresentative of a typical first-time user. The evaluation study will instead recruit a separate group of clinicians experienced in dementia assessment through the wider clinical network.

PHASE-1: CO-DESIGN FOCUS GROUP

A small group of clinicians and researchers (approximately 4 to 6\) from the CognoSpeak group will take part in the co-design phase. To accommodate clinicians' availability, rather than coordinating a single time window that suits everyone, this will be run as two focus group sessions of approximately 60 minutes each, with participants attending whichever session fits their schedule. Each session will be run as a hybrid meeting, with participants able to attend either in person or online via video conferencing (Google Meet). Participants will be shown an early version of the dashboard and asked open questions about its content, layout, clarity, and what information they would find useful in practice. With participants' consent, each session will be recorded to allow transcription. Because Google Meet captures audio and video together, the audio will be extracted and transcribed, and the video recording deleted once transcription is complete; only the anonymised transcripts are retained for analysis. The transcripts will be analysed using thematic analysis, a standard method for identifying recurring themes in qualitative data, complemented by some automated text-analysis methods (for example, computational analysis of word usage and recurring topics) to support and cross-check the thematic findings. Together these will inform the dashboard's design and may also be reported in academic publications in anonymised or aggregated form.

PHASE-2: FEEDBACK ROUND

Once a full draft of the dashboard is complete, members of the CognoSpeak group will be shown the draft and asked for structured feedback in a hybrid session, with participants able to attend either in person or online via video conferencing (Google Meet). With participants' consent, the session will be recorded to allow transcription. Because Google Meet captures audio and video together, the audio will be extracted and transcribed, and the video recording deleted once transcription is complete; only the anonymised transcript is retained for analysis. The feedback will be used to make final changes to the dashboard before it is frozen for the evaluation study, and will also be retained and analysed as research data using the same thematic and automated methods as Phase 1\. Feedback data may be reported in academic publications in anonymised or aggregated form. It will be stored securely and retained in line with University policy

PHASE-3: EVALUATION STUDY

Clinicians experienced in or familiar with the assessment of dementia will be recruited through the research team's clinical network. The core sample is specialists with direct diagnostic experience (e.g. speech and language therapists, geriatricians, neurologists, clinical psychologists). Resources permitting, recruitment will also extend to GPs and nurse practitioners working in primary care, as a stretch objective (see Objective 6): they are often the first point of contact for a possible dementia presentation, but may carry out this specific diagnostic task less routinely than specialists and may be less familiar with the assessment tools and reasoning involved, so it would be of interest to see how they engage with the dashboard. Participants will not include anyone who took part in the co-design or feedback phases.

The study will be completed online, at a time of the participant's choosing, so that clinicians can take part around their clinical commitments without needing to attend a scheduled session. The main study is delivered through a custom web application built by the research team in Python (using the Shiny for Python framework) and hosted on a University-managed virtual server requested from University IT Services, with HTTPS encryption and data-governance sign-off to be confirmed before recruitment begins. The application needs no separate database: each participant's progress and responses are written directly to a single per-participant file on the server, updated automatically as they work through the study, so no third-party platform is involved in delivering the study tasks or storing participants' responses.

Recruitment and consent proceed as follows. Potential participants are sent a link to a short online form (a Google Form hosted within the University's institutional Google Workspace). This form presents the participant information sheet and the consent statements, and asks the participant to type in their email address so that their personal study link can be sent to them; no Google account or sign-in is required to complete the form. A participant who wishes to take part indicates their consent by ticking the required statements and submitting the form. Submission triggers an automated process (a script bound to the form, requiring no manual action by the research team) which generates a unique, anonymous token for that participant, records it against their response for the research team's own records, and immediately emails the participant their personal study link, together with a copy of the information sheet and a record of the consent responses they just submitted, for their own records. Informed consent is therefore obtained, and recorded, before any study data is collected and before the participant receives the link to the study itself.

The participant then completes the main study by following their personal link to the web application on the University-managed virtual server. The study proceeds in the following order:

1\. On first entering the study application, the participant provides a small amount of professional information about themselves (their role, care setting, years of clinical experience, and how often they are involved in dementia assessment). These questions are a required part of taking part and are used to describe the participant group and to compare how different groups of clinicians use the dashboard.  
2\. The participant receives brief instructions and works through a practice case to become familiar with the dashboard before the main task begins.  
3\. The participant reviews a set of de-identified cases. Both which cases they see and the order in which they see them are derived automatically and deterministically from their personal study link, so the same participant always sees the same cases in the same order, and closing and reopening the link resumes the same sequence at the point they left off. For each case, the dashboard displays interpretable speech features across three views: an overview, a verbal-fluency view, and a picture-description view. For each case the participant records their judgement of whether the person is healthy, has mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or has dementia, and how confident they are in that judgement; the time taken to reach each decision is recorded automatically.  
4\. On completing the cases, the participant completes a short closing questionnaire, built into the same application, comprising a mix of Likert-scale and free-text items covering participants' feedback, usability, and other aspects of their experience using the dashboard.

The number of cases each participant reviews is provisionally set at 40, sampled from a held-out pool of 80 de-identified cases, stratified proportionally across the three diagnostic groups (healthy, MCI and dementia) to reflect their prevalence in that pool. This figure is a working estimate and will be confirmed with the CognoSpeak team, balancing the need for sufficient data for the comparison against the burden placed on participants; the final number will be fixed before recruitment begins and will not exceed the size of the held-out pool. The cases used are held out from, and not part of, the data used to train the automated classifiers, so that the comparison between clinicians and classifiers is fair.

The email address collected at the consent stage is used only to issue the personal study link and is held within the recruitment records (the linked Google Sheet), separately from the study responses. Participants' responses (their professional and demographic information, diagnostic judgements, confidence ratings and decision times) are stored in an individual, per-participant record on the University-hosted study server, not a third-party database or cloud service, written directly by the study application and accessible only to the research team via secure server access. Each record is keyed to an anonymous per-participant code rather than to any identifying information, and is not linked to the participant's email address or identity. No patient-identifiable information is presented to participants at any point, as the case data is de-identified.

ANALYSIS

The analysis combines quantitative and qualitative strands, in keeping with the mixed-methods design.

Diagnostic accuracy (primary outcome). The main outcome is diagnostic accuracy: how often each clinician's judgement (healthy, MCI or dementia) matches the confirmed diagnosis for each case. Because each clinician judges several cases and each case is judged by several clinicians, the data has a nested structure in which observations are not independent. This will be analysed using a statistical model appropriate to that structure, such as a mixed-effects (multilevel) model, which allows for variation between individual clinicians and between individual cases.

Reporting accuracy fairly across groups. Because the three groups differ in difficulty, with MCI being the hardest to identify, overall accuracy alone can be misleading. Accuracy will therefore also be summarised using measures better suited to unbalanced group difficulty, such as balanced accuracy or macro-averaged F1, alongside confusion matrices that show the pattern of correct and incorrect judgements across the three groups.

Confidence. The confidence ratings participants give for each judgement will be examined for calibration, that is, how well a clinician's stated confidence corresponds to how often they are actually correct.

Decision time. The time taken to reach each decision will be summarised and examined in relation to case difficulty and accuracy.

Comparison with automated classifiers. Clinicians' diagnostic accuracy will be compared against one or more automated classifier systems evaluated on the same held-out cases, using comparable accuracy measures, to understand the relative strengths of human and machine judgement, with particular attention to the difficult MCI group.

Questionnaire responses. The Likert-scale questionnaire responses, covering participants' experience of the dashboard's usability and related aspects, will be summarised using standard descriptive statistics.

Open-ended feedback. Free-text comments from the evaluation study, together with the transcripts from the co-design and feedback phases, will be analysed using thematic analysis to identify recurring themes, which may be complemented by computational text-analysis approaches to support and cross-check those themes.

Integration. The quantitative and qualitative findings will then be brought together to give an overall picture of how clinicians perform with the dashboard, how they experience using it, and how their judgement compares with automated classifiers.

## 5\. Potential Harm to Participants {#5.-potential-harm-to-participants}

*\* What is the potential for physical and/or psychological harm/distress to the participants?*  
The potential for harm to participants is low. There is no foreseeable risk of physical harm, as no phase of the research involves any physical procedure. The evaluation study is completed remotely by participants at a computer.

The main burden on participants is inconvenience and the time required to take part. A co-design or feedback session takes approximately 60 minutes. In the evaluation study, participants answer a short set of professional questions, work through brief instructions and a practice case, review a set of de-identified cases, and complete a short closing questionnaire. The case set is provisionally set at 40, sampled from an 80-case held-out pool and stratified proportionally across healthy, MCI and dementia presentations. This number will be confirmed before recruitment begins.

Any potential for psychological effect is minor. Two low-level possibilities have been considered. First, the evaluation study asks clinicians to make diagnostic judgements on which they receive no feedback, and which may be compared against automated systems. Some participants might feel some performance-related self-consciousness as a result. This is mitigated by the study design and framing: the study evaluates the dashboard, not the individual clinician, and no individual's performance is identified or reported. Second, in the co-design and feedback phases, participants are members of a small, identifiable research group and are known to the research team. This could in principle make some individuals cautious about giving feedback. This is addressed through anonymisation and the voluntariness measures described below.

The research does not address sensitive personal topics, does not involve vulnerable participants, and is not expected to elicit distressing disclosures. Participants engage in a professional capacity, responding to a software tool and to de-identified case data. No patient-identifiable material is shown to them at any point.

*\* How will this be managed to ensure appropriate protection and well-being of the participants?*  
Participation is entirely voluntary in all phases, and participants may withdraw at any time without giving a reason and without any consequence. This is stated clearly in the participant information sheet and consent process.

Participant burden is minimised by design. The evaluation study is completed online at a time of the participant's choosing, around their own commitments, rather than at a fixed scheduled time, and can be paused and resumed via the participant's personal link so that it need not be completed in one sitting. Co-design and feedback sessions are offered in a hybrid format and, for the co-design phase, across two alternative session times, so participants can take part in the way that is least disruptive to them.

To address performance-related self-consciousness in the evaluation study, the study is framed explicitly as an evaluation of the dashboard rather than of the individual clinician. No feedback on correctness is given during the task, individual participants are not identified in any analysis or output, and results are reported only in aggregated form. Moreover, each participant's responses are keyed to an anonymous code.

To support honest participation in the co-design and feedback phases, and given that these participants are colleagues of the research team, the voluntary nature of participation is made explicit and it is made clear that declining or withdrawing will have no effect on the individual's role, working relationships or standing within the group. Findings from these phases are reported only in anonymised or aggregated form, and any quotations used are anonymised.

All personal data is handled securely as set out in the personal-data section of this application, and no patient-identifiable data is presented to participants at any point. Standard University and departmental health and safety procedures apply to any sessions held on University premises. Should any participant raise a concern, it would be handled in line with the University's Preventing Harm in Research and Innovation (Safeguarding) Policy.

## 2\. Managing personal data {#2.-managing-personal-data}

*Who will have access to the personal data?*  
Access to personal data is limited to those who need it and is tiered according to how identifying the data is.

Identifiable personal data (participant email addresses, consent forms and records, and the session recordings and their transcripts from the co-design and feedback phases) will be accessible only to the immediate research team: the lead researcher Fritz Peters and their supervisor Heidi Christensen, within the School of Computer Science. This data is stored securely on University-managed systems and is not shared beyond the immediate research team.

De-identified research data may additionally be accessed by members of the CognoSpeak research group at the University of Sheffield, who are involved in the wider project. This is an interdisciplinary team spanning Computer Science, SITraN, and affiliated clinical collaborators. The de-identified data comprises the evaluation-study responses, which are keyed to anonymous participant codes, and the anonymised session transcripts. It also includes a small amount of professional and demographic information about each evaluation-study participant. This covers aspects such as their professional role, care setting, years of clinical experience, and how frequently they are involved in dementia assessment. This information is stored under the participant's anonymous code and separately from their email address. It contains no special category data. Because this data contains no identifying information, wider access within the project team does not affect participant confidentiality.

No personal data is shared with external organisations or third-party service providers. Audio from the session recordings is transcribed using Whisper, an open-source automatic speech recognition model, run locally on University-managed computing infrastructure by the research team. Because Whisper is run locally rather than as a cloud service, the recordings are not sent to, or processed by, any external transcription provider. The video recording is deleted once the audio has been transcribed, and only the anonymised transcript is retained.

*What measures, processes and/or agreements will be put in place to manage the personal data?*  
Minimising and separating identifiers: The evaluation study stores participants' responses under an anonymous per-participant code, held separately from the email address used for consent and for issuing the personal study link, so that the research dataset does not contain identifying information. Consent forms and records and participant contact details are kept separately from the research data.

Secure storage and limited access: All personal data is stored on university-managed systems. Evaluation-study responses are written directly to a per-participant file on the university-hosted study server (the application uses no separate database), not to any third-party or cloud service, and are accessible only to the immediate research team via secure server access. Identifiable data (contact details, consent forms, session recordings and transcripts) is accessible only to the lead researcher and supervisor. However, de-identified data may be accessed more widely by project collaborators, as set out above.

Anonymisation of recordings: Co-design and feedback sessions are recorded in Google Meet (covered by the University's institutional Google Workspace agreement) and transcribed locally using the open-source Whisper model on University-managed infrastructure, with no external transcription provider involved. The video recording is deleted once transcription is complete, and only anonymised transcripts are retained; identifying details are removed during transcription.

Protection in outputs. Participants will not be identifiable in any report, publication or other output. Findings are reported only in aggregate or anonymised form, and any quotations used are anonymised. Particular care is taken with the co-design and feedback phases, given the small, identifiable group involved, to ensure individuals cannot be identified from quoted or attributed material. The professional and demographic information collected from evaluation-study participants is gathered as banded, closed-response categories rather than free text, which reduces the risk of any individual being identifiable from a combination of characteristics. This information is analysed and reported only in aggregate, and never at a level of detail that could identify an individual participant.

Confidentiality and its limits: Participants are informed through the information sheet and consent process how their data will be kept confidential. Confidentiality would only be broken in the exceptional circumstance of a serious safeguarding or legal concern, handled in line with the University's relevant policies; this is made clear to participants in advance.

*When will the identifiable personal data be destroyed?*  
Identifiable personal data (contact details, consent records, and session recordings) is retained only as long as necessary and is destroyed within six months of the end of the project. The project is expected to end on 17 September 2027 (end of PhD), giving a destruction date of 17 March 2028\. Video recordings are deleted as soon as transcription is complete, well within that period. Fully anonymised research data, which is no longer personal data, may be retained and archived for verification, reuse and publication purposes.
